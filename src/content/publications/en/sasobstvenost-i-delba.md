Co-ownership most often arises through inheritance, joint purchase or after the end of a marriage. It brings practical difficulties: who uses the property, who pays the expenses, how decisions are taken. The Bulgarian Ownership Act and Inheritance Act provide the framework, and partition is the way to bring co-ownership to an end.

## 1. What it means to be a co-owner

Each co-owner holds an **undivided share** of the whole property, not a specific room or area. Shares are presumed equal unless otherwise follows from the title document or the law. A co-owner may use the property in proportion to their share without obstructing the others, and shares in the benefits and burdens in proportion to their share.

## 2. Decisions on management and use

Decisions on ordinary management are taken by a majority of shares, while more significant actions require the consent of all. Where use cannot be agreed, the court may allocate the actual use of the property among the co-owners. A co-owner who alone uses the whole property may owe compensation to the others after a written demand.

## 3. When partition may be requested

Any co-owner may request partition at any time, unless the law provides otherwise or partition is incompatible with the purpose of the property. The right to partition is not extinguished by limitation.

## 4. Voluntary partition

If the co-owners agree on how to divide, partition is carried out by **agreement**. For real estate the agreement must be in writing with notarised signatures and is registered in the property register. Voluntary partition is faster and considerably cheaper than judicial partition, but it requires the participation of **all** co-owners – a partition without one of them is void.

## 5. Judicial partition

In the absence of agreement, partition is carried out by the court in two phases:

1. **Admission of the partition** – the court establishes who the co-owners are, which properties are subject to partition and what the shares are.
2. **Carrying out the partition** – the properties are allocated among the parties.

The available methods in the second phase are: allocation of the properties into shares where they are divisible; equalisation of shares with money; sale of an indivisible property at public auction; or assignment of an indivisible dwelling to one of the parties under the conditions set by law.

## 6. Which documents are needed

- title documents (notarial deeds, contracts, court judgments);
- a certificate of heirs and a will, if any;
- a cadastral sketch or plan of the property and a tax assessment certificate;
- documents for improvements made and expenses paid, if they are to be claimed.

## 7. Practical tips

- Try voluntary partition first: it saves time, costs and relationships.
- Before judicial partition, obtain a valuation of the properties and shares so that you can realistically assess the possible outcomes.
- Be careful with claims for improvements and for use – they must be raised at a specific stage of the proceedings.
